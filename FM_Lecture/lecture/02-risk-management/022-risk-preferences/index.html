    Risk Preferences and Measures

    Up to now, we only presented some potential examples of risk measures and already saw the shortcomings of those towards some particular properties we deem as sound in terms of risk assessment. Furthremore, the selection might seem arbitrary. In the following we try to formalize what risk and uncertainty means.

    On the one hand, in some sense uncertainty is just about the fact that several outcomes might happen. In other terms this is akin to consider a set \(\Omega\) as in a probability space. The concept of uncertainty is therefore somehow an objective matter related to the nature of the world.

    On the other hand, risk is more of a subjective or personal perception towards uncertainty. It depends on oneself viewpoint and we want to model it as a subjective perception that is prudent towards uncertainty. The way to model it in a consistent mathematical way calls for the so called decision theory that models preferences among outcomes subject to uncertainty. In our context, we model uncertain outcomes as random variables, that is, we consider a vector space \(\mathcal{L}\) of random variables (for mathematical reason we mainly consider bounded).

    Definition: Preference Order and Numerical Representation

    A preference order \(\succcurlyeq\) on \(\mathcal{L}\) is a binary relation \(L_1 \succcurlyeq L_2\) that states \(L_1\) is preferred to \(L_2\). We assume this relation fulfills the following "rational" properties:

    • Transitivity: \(L_1 \succcurlyeq L_2\) and \(L_2 \succcurlyeq L_3\) implies \(L_1 \succcurlyeq L_3\);
    • Completeness: for any two random loss profiles, either \(L_1 \succcurlyeq L_2\) or \(L_2 \succcurlyeq L_1\).

    A function \(R\colon \mathcal{L} \to \mathbb{R}\) is called a numerical representation of a preference order \(\succcurlyeq\) if

    \[ L_1 \succcurlyeq L_2 \quad \text{if and only if} \quad R(L_1)\geq R(L_2) \]

    Preference orders are generic to represent the subjective view on those outcomes. A numerical representation is just a mapping ranking those preferences into \(\mathbb{R}\).

    Note

    Note first, that if we have a numerical representation \(R\) for a preference order \(\succcurlyeq\), it is not unique. Any strictly increasing function \(\phi \colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) will define another numerical representation \(\tilde{R} = \phi \circ R\). Indeed, \(L_1 \succcurlyeq L_2\) is equivalent to \(R(L_1)\geq R(L_2)\) which is equivalent to \(\phi(R(L_1)) = \tilde{R}(L_1) \geq \tilde{R}(L_2) = \phi(R(L_2))\).

    Second, starting directly with a function \(R \colon \mathcal{L} \to \mathbb{R}\) it defines a preference order \(\succcurlyeq\) by

    \[ L_1 \succcurlyeq L_2 : \Leftrightarrow R(L_1)\geq R(L_2) \]

    As an exercice, show that \(\succcurlyeq\) so defined through a function \(R\) is a preference order, that is, satisfies transitivity and completness.

    Third, even if giving a numerical function defines a preference order, the reciprocal is not necessarily true. Indeed, it requires some additional assumptions so that given a preference order you can find a numerical representation \(R\) of which. This is however most of the time the case under resonable assumptions.

    The first property tells you that if you find \(L_1\) more risky than \(L_2\) and \(L_2\) more risky than \(L_3\), then you should also find \(L_1\) more risky than \(L_3\). This property seems quite natural. The second property tells you that if you are given any two loss profiles, you are able to decide which one is the most risky of the two. This second property is actually quite strong as it requires you to be always able to decide among any two out of the infinite set \(\mathcal{L}\) which one is the more risky. These two rational (decision theorist would call them normative) assumptions are often falsified in empirical decision theory but these are meant to model a fully rational behavior in terms of decision towards these prospective outcomes. Nevertheless, these two properties do not really tell us about risk perception.

    Definition: Risk Order and Risk Measures

    A preference order \(\succcurlyeq\) on \(\mathcal{L}\) is called a risk order if the following two additional assumptions are fulfilled:

    • Diversification: if \(L_1\) is more risky than \(L_2\) then any diversified position between the two is less risky than the worse one:

      \[ \text{if } L_1 \succcurlyeq L_2 \quad \text{then}\quad L_1 \succcurlyeq \lambda L_1 + (1-\lambda) L_2, \quad \text{for every } 0 \leq \lambda \leq 1 \]

    • Worse for sure is more risky (monotonicity): if the loss of \(L_1\) are worse than the loss of \(L_2\) in any states of the world, then \(L_1\) is more risky than \(L_2\):

      \[ \text{if } L_1(\omega) \geq L_2 (\omega) \text{ for every } \omega \quad \text{then }\quad L_1 \succcurlyeq L_2 \]

    These two additional properties are expressing quite reasonable key features of what one might expect as a risk perception. They do have consequences in terms of resulting properties from a

    Proposition

    Let \(R\) be a numerical representation of a preference order \(\succcurlyeq\). Then the following assertions are equivalent:

    • \(\succcurlyeq\) is a risk order;
    • \(R\) is:
      • Quasi-convex: \(\max\{R(L_1), R(L_2)\} \geq R(\lambda L_1 + (1-\lambda) L_2)\) for every \(0 \leq \lambda \leq 1\);
      • Monotone: \(L_1(\omega) \geq L_2(\omega)\) for every \(\omega\) implies \(R(L_1) \geq R(L_2)\).
    Proof

    Let \(L_1\) and \(L_2\) be two loss profiles. Assume that \(\succcurlyeq\) is a risk order. As for the quasi-convexity, due to the completeness of the relation, we may assume without loss of generality that \(L_1 \succcurlyeq L_2\) which is equivalent to \(R(L_1) = \max\{R(L_1), R(L_2)\} \geq R(L_2)\). Now for every \(0 \leq \lambda \leq 1\) it follows from the diversification property that \(L_1 \succcurlyeq \lambda L_1 + (1-\lambda) L_2\) which according to Relation implies

    \[ \max\{R(L_1), R(L_2)\} = R(L_1) \geq R(\lambda L_1 + (1-\lambda) L_2) \]

    showing quasi-convexity of \(R\). As for the monotonicity, assume that \(L_1(\omega) \geq L_2(\omega)\) for every \(\omega\). It follows from the worse for sure assumption that \(L_1 \succcurlyeq L_2\) which by means of Relation implies that

    \[ R(L_1) \geq R(L_2) \]

    showing the monotonicity of \(R\).

    The fact that a numerical representation is quasi-convex and monotone implies that \(\succcurlyeq\) is a risk order follows the reverse argumentation since Relation is an if and only if statement.
